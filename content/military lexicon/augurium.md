# augurium

The **augury** — and not a neutral word. In Rome the *augurium* is the response of the *collegium augurum*, the priestly science on which every public and military act depended: the city itself was founded on the *augurium* of Romulus' twelve vultures, and the chain *augere → augur → augustus* ties the word to the very title of the emperors. Two occurrences in Numbers, both in the Balaam cycle.

## Textual tradition

| TM (Hebrew) | LXX | Hexaplaric | Vulgate | Occurrences |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| נַחַשׁ — *naḥaš*, "omen(-reading)" | οἰωνισμός (23,23); τοῖς οἰωνοῖς (24,1) | — | **augurium** | 2 in Numbers (full text, verified) |

[[Liber Numerorum/23|Nm 23,23]]: "non est **augurium** in Iacob nec divinatio in Israhel" — the oracle that defines Israel by subtraction. The Hebrew *naḥaš* is the observing of omens, and the LXX's οἰωνισμός (bird-omen) already points toward the sky; but by choosing the marked Roman institution — *augurium*, not the commoner *auspicium* — the Vulgate has Balaam deny to Jacob precisely the science that founded Rome's ethnogenesis and legitimated her commanders. A people without augury is a people whose wars need no college of diviners: its command comes by [[imperium]] Domini. At [[Liber Numerorum/24|Nm 24,1]] Balaam himself stops going "ut **augurium** quaereret" (Hebrew "to meet the omens") once he sees that blessing pleases God. A Roman category with no direct Hebrew counterpart; Jerome's own Hebrew text (unvocalized, possibly non-Masoretic) is unknown — the alignment is ours.
