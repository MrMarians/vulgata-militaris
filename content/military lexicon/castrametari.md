# castrametari

To lay out a camp by measurement — the technical verb of Roman military surveying, performed
by the *metatores* and *mensores* before the troops arrive. Jerome uses it, in the univerbated
form (*castrametati sunt*) and the separated one (*castra metari*, *metabuntur*), **39 times
in Numbers**: the whole wilderness itinerary becomes a systematic *castrametatio*.

## Textual tradition

| TM (Hebrew) | LXX | Hexaplaric | Vulgate | Occurrences |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| חנה — *ḥānāh*, "to encamp" (generic) | παρεμβάλλω (uniform) | — | **castrametari / castra metari** | 39 in Numbers (full text, verified) |

The Hebrew verb is semantically plain — "to camp, to halt" — and the LXX keeps it plain with
παρεμβάλλω. The Old Latin witnesses oscillate (*castra ponere*, *figere tentoria*, *manere*),
though the *castrametati sunt* refrain of ch. 33 is already attested in them. What Jerome does
is make the **geometrical** term systematic: in Roman usage *castra metari* is the surveyors'
operation codified by Pseudo-Hyginus and Vegetius ("in metandis castris non sufficit locum
bonum legere", *Epit. rei mil.* III,8) and still alive in Ammianus ("castra metata"). Israel's
camp is not pitched; it is **measured**. Jerome himself flags the choice: "quia de exercitu
dicitur, *castra* transtulimus" (Ep. 78,1). Since the underlying Hebrew has no surveying
nuance, this is a Roman technicism without a direct Hebrew counterpart; and what Hebrew text
Jerome actually read here is unknown (an unvocalized consonantal text, possibly at points
non-Masoretic), so the alignment is ours, not his.

At [[Liber Numerorum/34|Nm 34,10]] the same verb marks the tracing of a **border**
(*metabuntur fines*, for a different Hebrew verb, hitp. of ʾwh "to mark out"): camp-laying and
boundary-drawing are one and the same *ars gromatica*.
