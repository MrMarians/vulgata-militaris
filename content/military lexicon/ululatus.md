# ululatus

The **howl** — in classical Latin the battle-yell of the Gauls ("suo more victoriam conclamant atque ululatum tollunt", Caesar, *BG* V,37), the funeral wail, the orgiastic cry of the Maenads in Vergil. An absolute novelty of Jerome's Numbers: none of the older versions dared it for the sacred alarm-blast.

## Textual tradition

| TM (Hebrew) | LXX | Hexaplaric | Vulgate | Occurrences |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| תְּרוּעָה — *tərûʿâ*; hiphil of רוע *rûaʿ* | σημασία (a flat "signal") | **Aq. ἀλαλαγμός** — the war-whoop | **ululatus / ululare** | 4 in Numbers (full text, verified) |

The *tərûʿâ* is a polyvalent sacral signal — war-cry, departure-blast, Jubilee sound — whose vibrating clamour summons the divine presence. The LXX neutralizes it (σημασία, "signal"); but Aquila's ἀλαλαγμός, the Greek battle-yell, is preserved in the hexaplaric tradition, and it is the closest antecedent of Jerome's choice. *Ululatus* imports a disturbing, warlike sound-quality: the sacral blast of Israel now sounds like the war-howl the Latin reader knew from barbarian battle-lines (Ammianus: "barbarico ululans"). All four occurrences sit in the signal ordinance of Nm 10, always of the **broken** blast that moves the camp and sounds the alarm — the camp departs not to a rite of presence but to a mobilization signal. As with every such choice, what Hebrew text Jerome read is unknown (unvocalized, possibly non-Masoretic); the alignment is ours.
