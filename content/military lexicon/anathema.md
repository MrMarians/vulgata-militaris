# anathema

The **devoted thing** — the Greek sacral loanword (ἀνάθεμα, "what is set apart") that the Septuagint fixed as the equivalent of Hebrew *ḥērem*, the war-ban that consecrates the enemy to destruction. One occurrence in Numbers, and it is the keystone of the book's divine-war lexicon.

## Textual tradition

| TM (Hebrew) | LXX | Hexaplaric | Vulgate | Occurrences |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| חָרְמָה — the toponym *Ḥormâ*, from the root ḥ-r-m | Ἀνάθεμα (the place-name itself translated away) | — | **Horma id est anathema** | 1 in Numbers; the noun ḥērem at 18,14 → *votum* |

[[Liber Numerorum/21|Nm 21,3]]: Israel vows the ban ([[delere]], v. 2), executes it ([[interficere]], [[subvertere]]), "et vocavit nomen loci illius **Horma, id est anathema**." The LXX had dissolved the Hebrew name into its Greek gloss (Ἀνάθεμα, and the Old Latin followed); Jerome **restores the toponym** and attaches the gloss — hebraica veritas and inherited sacral vocabulary in a single phrase. The distribution of the ban in his Numbers is now complete: the **act** goes to plain killing-verbs (*delebo* 21,2, *interfecit* 21,3), the **object** keeps the Greek sacral name (*anathema*), and the priestly-due sense of ḥērem at [[Liber Numerorum/18|Nm 18,14]] becomes a *votum*. What Jerome never does is reach for the Roman categories that fitted the rite almost exactly — the *devotio* by which a general consigned an enemy city to the gods of the dead, the double-edged *sacer*: to Romanise the ban would have subordinated Israel's holy war to Rome's infernal gods. To Latinise is not always to Romanise. In the Church's later speech the word travelled far: *anathema sit* (Gal 1,8-9) became the formula of excommunication. For the defeat at the same place, see [[persequi]] (14,45).
