# seditio

**Sedition** — in Roman law and Roman fear, the crime of the mutinous assembly: the legions' *seditiones* of the historians, the urban uprisings of the codes. Five occurrences in Numbers, every one an interpretive rendering: no Hebrew word "means" sedition.

## Textual tradition

| TM (Hebrew) | LXX | Hexaplaric | Vulgate | Occurrences |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| עֵדָה — *ʿēdâ* "the community (of Korah)" (26,9; 27,3); קהל niphal "assemble against" (16,42); ריב *rîb* "quarrel" (20,3); דְּבַר־קֹרַח "the affair of Korah" (16,49) | συναγωγή (mechanical for ʿēdâ); ἐπισυνίστημι ("rise up together") for the Korah band | — | **seditio** | 5 in Numbers (full text, verified) |

Where the Hebrew sees an *assembly* — the same ʿēdâ that elsewhere is the sacred congregation — and the LXX keeps its one word for it (συναγωγή), the Vulgate reads the **quality** of the gathering: assembled *against*, it is a *seditio*. So the swarm after Korah's death ("cumque oreretur **seditio**", [[Liber Numerorum/16|Nm 16,42]] = MT 17,7), the "affair of Korah" itself (16,49), and the *ʿădat Qōraḥ* remembered twice in the second census ([[Liber Numerorum/26|Nm 26,9]]; [[Liber Numerorum/27|Nm 27,3]] — where Zelophehad's daughters clear their father of it). At Meriba even the people's *rîb*, their juridical quarrel over water, becomes "versi in **seditionem**" ([[Liber Numerorum/20|Nm 20,3]]): grievance framed as mutiny. For a fourth-century reader *seditio* is what armies do to emperors — the camp of Israel gets the discipline-vocabulary of the camp of Rome. See [[proceres]] for the aristocratic coloring of the ringleaders, and [[multitudo]] / [[synagoga]] / [[congregatio]] for the same lemma ʿēdâ where the gathering is not hostile.
