# hostis

The **public enemy** — in Roman usage *hostis* is the enemy of the community, the foreign foe of the state, as against *inimicus*, one's personal enemy. The Vulgate of Numbers keeps that legal distinction with surprising rigor.

## Textual tradition

| TM (Hebrew) | LXX | Hexaplaric | Vulgate | Occurrences |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| צַר / צרר — *ṣar*, the pressing foe (10,9; 24,8; 25,17); added at 14,31 | ὑπεναντίοι / ἐχθροί; 25,17 ἐχθραίνετε | — | **hostis** | 4 in Numbers (full text, verified) |

The distribution is systematic: Hebrew **ṣar** ("the presser, oppressor") goes to *hostis*, Hebrew **ʾōyēb** ("the hater") to [[inimicus]] — and the proof-verse is [[Liber Numerorum/10|Nm 10,9]], where both stand side by side: "contra **hostes** (ṣar) qui dimicant adversum vos… ut eruamini de manibus **inimicorum** (ʾōyēb) vestrorum." Two Hebrew enemy-words, two Latin enemy-words, correctly paired. The other loci: the nations the warrior-God devours (24,8, *ṣārāyw*); the command to treat Midian as enemies — "**hostes** vos sentiant Madianitae" (25,17, the infinitive absolute of *ṣārar* made a declaration of hostility); and 14,31, where "your children, who you said would be booty" gains an explicit *hostibus* — the enemy supplied by the translator. What Hebrew text Jerome read is unknown; the alignments are ours.
